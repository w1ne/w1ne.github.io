In plain words

How it works — no engineering background needed

The whole idea in four steps, then one honest line about what it can't claim.

1

The fish are already moving

On a fishing boat, small fish like anchovies are pumped through pipes in water, by the thousand. FlowStun is just a short, smart section of that pipe — nothing has to be handled one by one.

2

A gentle electric field, for a moment

As the fish pass through, the unit sends a controlled electrical field through the water — the same idea already used to stun farmed salmon humanely, re-engineered for fast-moving small fish.

3

They stop feeling, at once

Done right, the fish become unconscious immediately and stay that way until the next step — chilling or bleeding — ends their life. No thrashing, no slow suffocation on ice.

4

The machine checks itself

Sensors confirm every fish got the right dose. If anything drifts, it raises a flag or safely lets fish pass straight through, so the boat's work never stops.

What it does not do: it never claims a sensor can read a fish's mind. Scientists in a welfare lab prove the fish are genuinely unconscious; the machine's job is to deliver that proven recipe to every fish — and keep a record proving it did.

Problem definition

Mass handling creates a welfare bottleneck.

Small pelagic fish are handled in dense, fast-moving flows. Existing slaughter and capture handling practices often prioritize speed, chilling, and deck logistics over controlled pre-death insensibility.

Producing an electric pulse is the easy part. The hard part is landing a reliable dose in saltwater, on a moving boat, at full throughput, while fish density, size, water conductivity and electrode fouling all keep shifting.

▦

Huge volumes

One-at-a-time handling does not fit high-volume pelagic operations. The intervention needs continuous flow or scalable parallel lanes.

△

Welfare uncertainty

Motion cessation, chilling, and apparent immobility are not sufficient proof of insensibility. A valid solution needs biological endpoints.

≋

Vessel constraints

It has to survive saltwater, washdown, corrosion and vibration in tight deck space — without adding crew work or slowing the unload.

Validation strategy

The lab proves it works. The machine proves it happened.

The deployed unit never claims its sensors prove a fish is unconscious — that's the lab's job. The unit only proves the fish got the dose the lab signed off on.

Lab validation

Scientific proof

  • Controlled live-fish studies determine insensibility and absence of recovery.
  • Behavioral, neurophysiological, and physiological endpoints are specified by a qualified welfare partner.
  • The humane operating envelope is species-, size-, water-, and condition-specific.

Important distinction

Motion cessation and impedance changes alone do not prove consciousness has been abolished.

Lab studies generate the evidence. The deployed system verifies that exposure stayed inside the validated envelope.

Field proxy verification

Delivery assurance

  • Current, voltage, impedance, residence time, water conductivity, and faults are logged.
  • Out-of-envelope events trigger alarms, auto-adjustment, or bypass.
  • Batch logs support auditability and continuous improvement.

Experimental plan

Twelve months, four gated phases.

No live animals until the electronics are proven, the welfare protocol is set, and the prototype has passed safety testing.

Phase 1 — Non-live engineering characterization

Months 1–3

  • Map water conductivity and load ranges.
  • Build electrode cassette and bench flow chamber.
  • Validate sensing, isolation, control loops, and fault response.
  • Select two or three electrode geometries for biological testing.

Phase 2 — Partner protocol and reduced live-search space

Months 4–6

  • Primary species: European anchovy.
  • Backup species: European pilchard/sardine.
  • Define endpoints, sample sizes, stopping rules, animal-use approvals, and downstream death method.
  • Narrow the live-trial matrix to pre-specified exposure envelopes.

Phase 3 — Controlled live-fish validation

Months 7–9

  • Test pre-specified envelopes only in an approved facility.
  • Measure immediate insensibility, recovery risk, handling harm, and engineering stability.
  • Stop if no humane envelope emerges inside safe and practical limits.

Phase 4 — Vessel-realistic pilot design

Months 10–12

  • Harden the module for washdown, corrosion, vibration, mounting, connector abuse, and cleaning.
  • Target a 1–2 tonne/hour/lane pilot concept with parallel-lane scaling.
  • Deliver BOM, SOPs, risk register, cost model, and full-proposal plan.

Risk analysis

The ways this can fail — said out loud.

Filter the main failure modes. Better to name them than bury them.

High

Sensor overclaim

Mitigation: explicitly use sensors as delivery proxies only; lab endpoints define humane envelope.

High

Nonuniform exposure

Mitigation: electrode cassette testing, CFD/flow studies, residence-time sensing, and batch-level fault logging.

Med

Saltwater safety and corrosion

Mitigation: isolation, sealed connectors, ground-fault detection, marine-rated materials, and washdown testing.

Med

Recovery before death

Mitigation: validation includes duration of insensibility and downstream timing to bleed/chill.

High

Operator rejects downtime

Mitigation: bypass mode, low pressure drop, easy electrode replacement, and cleaning-time constraints.

Med

No named partner

Mitigation: secure fish welfare lab and process/vessel contact before final submission.
